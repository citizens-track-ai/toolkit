Artificial intelligence discussions often default to Anglo-American examples and English technical jargon. For community assemblies to be genuinely rooted, local facilitators are strongly encouraged to translate and localise materials for their communities.

---

## 1. Localising Examples & Case Studies

When presenting background information:

- **Replace Global Examples with Local Stories:** If an activity mentions autonomous delivery vehicles or facial recognition in London or San Francisco, replace it with examples from your country or city (e.g. biometric welfare distribution, agricultural crop monitoring, automated bus routing, or local call-centre automation).
- **Match Community Realities:** In rural or agricultural regions, emphasise topics like water rights, seasonal labour monitoring, or localized weather prediction over Silicon Valley generative chatbots.
- **Engage with Local Law & Culture:** Reference your national data protection legislation, indigenous data sovereignty principles, or regional constitution rather than distant foreign statutes.

---

## 2. Navigating Technical Terminology

Many AI concepts lack direct translations or sound overly academic in everyday speech:

- **Create a Shared Community Glossary:** Spend five minutes at the start of your session agreeing on how your group will refer to key concepts in your language (e.g. *machine learning*, *algorithm*, *training data*, *hallucination*).
- **Use Everyday Metaphors:** Explain algorithms as "recipes," predictive text as "auto-complete suggestions on your phone," and datasets as "a library of past examples."
- **Avoid Mystification:** Avoid terms that personify AI as thinking, conscious beings (e.g. "the AI decided to fire the worker"). Keep human agency and company decisions visible (e.g. "the management used an algorithmic scoring tool to dismiss employees").

---

## 3. The Multilingual Toolkit Workflow

The Citizens’ Track on AI Toolkit is built with native internationalisation (`i18n`) support in Hugo and Sveltia CMS:

1. **Available Languages:** The toolkit is actively maintained in English (`en`), Spanish (`es`), French (`fr`), and German (`de`).
2. **Offline Print Formats:** Editable card templates are provided in open-source vector formats (SVG and Markdown) so local facilitators can print translated versions with zero software licensing costs.
3. **Sharing Your Adaptations:** When you translate a card deck or module into a new language or dialect, please share your files with [contact@citizens-track.org](mailto:contact@citizens-track.org). We will publish your translation to the open repository to support facilitators worldwide.
