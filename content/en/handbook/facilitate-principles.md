Every activity, card deck, and workshop module in the Citizens’ Track on AI Toolkit is built upon five foundational design principles. When adapting materials or creating new activities, keep these principles at the center:

---

## Principle 1: Everyone Can Have a Say on the Future of AI

You do not need a computer science degree or deep technical expertise to hold legitimate, powerful views on how artificial intelligence should operate in your society.

> ### The Electricity System Analogy
>
> “I don’t fully understand how the national electricity grid works in technical detail… but I know that I want a reliable, safe, sustainable, and affordable electricity supply. 
>
> No one should tell me I am not allowed to demand this simply because I don’t know a substation from a transformer circuit. 
>
> Now, if I want to get involved in making technical engineering plans to upgrade our local power grid, I might need to learn more specifics. 
>
> In the exact same way, every citizen has the right to set the social, ethical, and legal boundaries for AI—even if they do not know how to train a neural network.”

Understanding technical mechanisms is helpful for designing solutions, but democratic legitimacy begins with citizens articulating the values and standards they expect technology to uphold.

---

## Principle 2: Everyone Brings Vital Insights & Lived Experience

No one comes to an assembly as an empty vessel. Whether as parents navigating educational software, factory workers facing algorithmic monitoring, patient advocates dealing with healthcare triage, or artists protecting their craft—**participants bring essential knowledge about how systems function in the real world.**

Assemblies must structure discussions so that lived experience is valued just as highly as expert technical input.

---

## Principle 3: AI May Change Many Things… But Fundamental Rights Remain

Artificial intelligence may present novel technological dilemmas, but the foundational architecture of human rights and collective stewardship does not disappear:

- **Universal Human Rights:** The *Universal Declaration of Human Rights* established inalienable rights to human dignity, bodily autonomy, liberty, equality, freedom of thought, peaceful assembly, fair labour, health, housing, and education. These rights apply unconditionally in the age of algorithms.
- **Rule of Law:** While national and international laws must be updated to address AI harms, legal accountability, civil liability, and democratic oversight cannot be bypassed under the guise of "technological inevitability."
- **Our Finite Planet:** We all share one finite Earth. AI models rely on vast physical infrastructures—water for cooling, rare earth minerals, and enormous electrical grids. Discussions about AI are inextricably linked to climate justice and ecological limits.

---

## Principle 4: Aiming for Common Ground, Not Forced Consensus

Civic assemblies should not force participants into artificial compromises. Different communities have genuinely distinct interests and worldviews.

Our objective is to discover **meaningful common ground**—identifying shared principles, areas of broad agreement, and clearly documenting differing perspectives with clarity and mutual respect.

## Content Architecture & Authoring Standards

To maintain academic rigor and global accessibility, every piece of toolkit content follows strict content design criteria:

- **Clear Information Typing:** Content separates verified *Statements*, plain-English *Explanations*, cited *Statistics*, and actionable *Take-aways*.
- **Perspectives & Mental Models:** Rather than presenting tech as static, content showcases diverse viewpoints and aids participants in building conceptual mental models (technical, economic, and social).
- **Mandatory Citations:** Every factual statement or statistic must include a traceable, authoritative source citation.
- **Review & Validation Workflow:** Content items display peer-review metadata, including named reviewer approval, review dates, and review notes.
- **Adaptation Annotations:** Content items include contextual notes guiding international facilitators on how to localise concepts without losing their core deliberative intent.
