The Citizens' Track on AI // Community Assembly Toolkit

Community Assembly Toolkit

A structured, practical guide and modular toolkit to prepare, facilitate, and follow up community assemblies and civic deliberations on artificial intelligence.

“A community assembly is a structured, inclusive space where people come together to discuss issues affecting their communities, learn from one another, and develop practical actions to create positive impact.

Unlike a typical public meeting, a community assembly is designed to:
• Bring together a diversity of perspectives and lived experiences
• Create space for respectful dialogue and shared learning
• Include people who are often excluded from decision-making
• Turn deliberation into robust, concrete action

Community assemblies are grounded in the belief that communities already hold valuable knowledge about the challenges they face. By combining lived experience with shared reflection and practical planning, assemblies can generate solutions that are more relevant, trusted, and effective.”

Source: Assemblis Community Assembly Guide
Diverse Participation Assemblies intentionally bring together people with different backgrounds, ages and perspectives - especially those who are often excluded from traditional decision making spaces.
Facilitated Dialogue Assemblies are carefully facilitated to ensure discussion remains respectful, constructive and inclusive.
Action-Oriented Outcomes Assemblies are not only spaces for discussion. They are designed to produce practical next steps, recommendations, partnerships, campaigns, or community-led actions.

Foundational Assumptions for AI Assemblies

• AI is a vast topic. There are many different issues that communities want to talk about.
• Many people do not feel that they are well informed about AI. There is a need for learning and capacity building.
• Everyone, regardless of their background, can bring experience and insights to discussions about AI.

Five Questions to Prepare for Your AI Assembly

Question 1

How are you going to recruit participants?

  • Run the assembly with an existing group
  • Send targeted invitations
  • Run an open call
  • Create a ‘mini-public’ with sortition / representative selection
Question 2

How will you frame your assembly?

  • As a space to explore community attitudes towards AI
  • As an opportunity to make recommendations to decision makers
  • In response to a particular event or issue
Question 3

What topics do you want to address?

⏱️ Allow four hours for an assembly on one topic, and at least two hours for each additional topic.

  • 1. AI: Hopes and Fears
  • 2. AI Safety & Security
  • 3. Infrastructure & Data Centres
  • 4. Language and Diversity
  • 5. AI Governance
Question 4

What resources will you need?

✓ A host organisation
✓ Venue
✓ Refreshments
✓ Facilitators
✓ Notetakers
✓ Travel support
✓ Accessibility support
✓ Participant stipends / recognition
Question 5

What could happen with the outputs from your assembly?

  • Support the community to plan follow-up actions
  • Add to the PAVE Data Commons as evidence of community views
The more detail you can provide on your community assembly, its outputs and outcomes, the more we can help amplify your messages.

Community Assembly on AI Toolkit Principles

⚡

Everyone should be able to have a say on the future of AI

You don’t need to understand everything about how a system works to have a view on what it should do. (But some understanding will help when you want to create change…) This is true for AI systems… and of political or social systems too.

The Electricity Analogy:
“For example: I don’t fully understand how the electricity system works… but I know I want a reliable, safe and affordable electricity supply. No-one should tell me I can’t ask for this because I don’t know a substation from a circuit. But, if I want to get involved in making detailed plans to improve power supply, I might need to learn a bit more detail.”
🌍

AI may change a lot… but a lot is unchanged unless people choose to make change

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights established inalienable rights of people to dignity, liberty, equality, freedom of thought, associations and assembly, and to economic, social and cultural rights goods like the right to work, health, housing and education.

National and international laws may have to deal with new situations, but they don’t just go away when AI technologies arrive. We all still share one finite planet on which we find different ways of living, draw on different cultures, and participate in many different forms of community.

🤝

We don’t have to find consensus, but we are aiming for common ground

Everyone can bring insights and experience that will help us to form views on AI. We are creating materials that can be used flexibly with groups at different levels of knowledge and experience.

Stage 3: Follow-up & Voice to Impact

Recording deliberation outcomes, feeding into the PAVE Data Commons, and linking to national and global AI consultations.

Read Chapter →
About the PAVE Data Commons

PAVE stands for Public AI Voice and Engagement. The PAVE Data Commons is a resource that documents a wide range of participatory processes that have gathered community voices on AI.

If your assembly outcomes and recommendations are recorded in the PAVE Data Commons then they could:
• Feature on the PAVE map of engagement shown to decision-makers at key AI governance events around the world;
• Feed into policy briefs, consultation responses and reports highlighting public priorities for the future of AI;
• Form part of the evidence and advocacy work of the Citizens Track on AI.

Questions and Outputs

Scope: Personal | Collective | National | Global

Output Format Scope Description / Examples
Recommendations Collective or Individual Practical next steps, policy guidance, or principles agreed upon by participants during the action session.
Statements Personal or Group Key messages reflecting diverse lived experiences, concerns, or expectations of community members.
Consultation Responses Collective / National / Global Structured submissions responding to open government consultations, international policy drafts, or summit inquiries.
Surveys & Polling Individual / Group Quantitative and qualitative responses captured via dot-voting, digital survey forms, or paper feedback cards.

Options on Global Deliberation on AI

Domain 1

Values and Vision

How do we live good lives alongside AI?
Principles and policy recommendations to promote human flourishing alongside AI, including in relation to the world of work.

What values should be built into all generative AI systems?
Perspectives on whether publics prefer monolithic AI that represents all cultures and worldviews, or many localized systems that work differently depending on context.

How can AI best deliver sustainable development and public good?
Public priorities for AI funding, effort and alignment; suggested moratoria on anti-social uses of AI.

Domain 2

Governance and Regulation

What global rules should govern AI?
Proposed common rules for policy makers or firms to follow. Draft treaty on AI governance.

What control should local communities have over AI?
Reporting on regional and national variation with respect to key AI governance and alignment questions.

What should the design of an AI Safety Assessment take into account?
Public priorities to guide AI Safety Institutes; intercultural validation of assessment frameworks; review of international AI standards.

Should we allow Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS)?
Citizens declaration on use of AI in lethal autonomous weapons systems. Input to global standards for use of LAWS.

How should data be governed in order to deliver equitable AI?
Recommendations on data governance and management to mitigate risks and secure benefits of AI.

Domain 3

Applications and Implementation

How should a chatbot respond to questions about contentious issues?
Principles to guide AI system training, statements for LLM fine-tuning, or raw transcripts representing diversity of perspectives to be used in LLM training.

How should AI be used within participatory or democratic decision making?
Guidelines and mandate for use of AI systems within the wider global participatory processes.

When is it appropriate for AI to be used to make decisions?
Application- (and context-) specific guidance on issues like health, law, education etc.

Target Consultations & Global Opportunities

Active Opportunity

UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance (UNGD)

Who is asking? We expect that the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance (UNGD) will run an open consultation process. They are likely to ask about priority themes and actions that should be discussed at the next meeting of the UNGD in New York in May 2027.

What’s the focus? The write-up from the first UNGD identified 12 themes as “a basis for continued exchange and practical cooperation”.
What are the questions?
• What should the UN Global Dialogue prioritise in 2027?
• Which AI issues demand strict, non-negotiable legal red lines that apply across the whole world, and which should be addressed through adaptive, voluntary standards that might vary by country or region?
How to share your views:
• Direct submission if an open consultation is available.
• The Citizens’ Track on AI team submission based on input from community assemblies.
• National delegations to the UN Global Dialogue.
Feedback Tools:
1. Share your priority from existing themes or suggest additional themes (Linked activity: prioritisation).
2. Write five to ten recommendations for the UN Global Dialogue in your own words (Linked activity: drafting a declaration).
Consultation Framework

Make.org Consultation Questions

Four foundational questions exploring public sentiment and safeguarding priorities.

1. How should our societies prepare for AI's disruption of work, especially for young people?
What we will look for: which transitions worry citizens most, and what support they expect (training, social protection, new pathways into work).
2. How should we rethink education to benefit from AI without becoming dependent on it?
What we will look for: which skills still matter, where AI belongs in learning and where it does not, and how to preserve independent thinking.
3. How can we protect human creativity in the age of AI?
What we will look for: what citizens value in human-made work, and which protections they want for creators (consent, fair pay, transparency).
4. Concentration of power, great-power rivalry, artificial general intelligence: what minimal international safeguards does AI need?
What we will look for: which risks citizens see as most serious, and the minimum rules they want agreed at global level.
