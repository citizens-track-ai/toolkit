#01

The AI Race

by Gloria Mendoza

🎨 Artist's Statement
The AI race describes the companies and nations which are racing forward to the newest iteration of 'AI', including software, hardware capacity, power, leadership, and influence in the field. It's a race that nation leaders and private corporations have signed up for, but workforces, neighbourhoods, communities, and working class people are impacted by the harms caused. I strive to show the implications of technology, in this case, the runner represents the workers running the race for entire nations, and its silhouette contains the materials, hardware, and resources (often hidden) needed to build generative AI technologies. Skylines are representative of cities and progress, but the heavy smoke cruising along the image representing the pollution, e-waste, and debris the race leaves behind. The panels of colour represent the countries typically referred to in the context of the "AI race": the US and China. But I leave this open to interpretation and did not want the flags and countries to distract from the most important aspect of the illustration: the consequences of the AI race.

#02

Rare Metals 2

by Hanna Barakat & Archival Images of AI + AIxDESIGN

🎨 Artist's Statement
'Rare Metals' highlights the human labor that underpins the material realities of modern technology. Archival images of hands — some cast in cement, others hand-drawn — symbolise the toxic burden of data cleaning in China and the immense human cost of mineral extraction in the Congo. Some hands are ghostlike, their translucence reflecting the often-invisible nature of this labor. Others delicately manipulate threads of rare earth metals, essential for manufacturing computer chips. By using hands and strings as visual metaphors, the series seeks to uncover the hidden stories of "anonymous" labor that fuel AI technologies.

#03

Frontier Models 1

by Hanna Barakat & Archival Images of AI + AIxDESIGN

🎨 Artist's Statement
This image explores the visual culture of generative artificial intelligence landscapes, positioning it as a "new frontier" that echoes the 1850s to 1870s Manifest Destiny paintings (often associated with the Hudson River School). The base image, titled “Across the Continent: Westward the Course of Empire Takes Its Way" by Frances Flora Bond Palmer (1868) is one of many propagandistic artworks that intended to lure settlers westward while “conveniently” concealing the ethnic cleansing of Indigenous populations. Through collage, this image reappropriates the idea of ‘the next frontier’ as AI-frontier models to draw attention to the not-so-new forms of colonisation within the digital realm.

#07

Data annotators labeling data

by Nacho Kamenov & Humans in the Loop

🎨 Artist's Statement
Data annotation is a key activity required in order to prepare training data for Artificial Intelligence. Manual data annotation is especially needed when dealing with unstructured data: images, videos, and text. For example, if we want to train an AI model to recognize animals on images, we will need the help of data annotators who would draw bounding boxes or add tags to each image depending on what animal it depicts. Data annotation work is being performed by hundreds of workers around the world who use the job as a means of livelihoods.

Artist(s) Reihaneh Golpayegani & The Bigger Picture
#11

A Corner Of The History

by Reihaneh Golpayegani & The Bigger Picture

🎨 Artist's Statement
This digital painting was created using Procreate. The image shows a glowing wall on the left filled with recognisable app icons like Netflix, Spotify, Alexa, and OpenAI. A trial of footsteps converges towards these icons, symbolising our attention and fascination towards AI. On the opposite side, various physical items, like books, vintage records, a rotary telephone, and paintings, are shrouded in a shadow. Despite this, these objects occupy more space, representing both their substantial historical impact and their persistent relevance in our increasingly digital world. The juxtaposition between the shiny icons and these shadowed historical objects invites us to adopt a retrospective and prospective view towards AI, acknowledging AI’s shiny surface and its deeper historical roots. The footsteps that are veering in the opposite direction symbolises the uncertainties and open questions about the future of AI and technology. It suggests that while AI might dominate our focus today, understanding its evolution requires a broader perspective that includes past mediums and future unknowns.

#18

Colossal Harvest

by Hanna Barakat & Cambridge Diversity Fund

🎨 Artist's Statement
'Colossal Harvest' combines the history of UK computing with a critical reading of diversity in AI development. A young, anonymous, woman is juxtaposed with a digital image of the Colossus machine, an early computer that helped British cryptologists like Alan Turing decipher Germany’s Enigma-encrypted messages during World War II. While approximately 7,500 women worked in Bletchley Park to operate the computers used for code-breaking decryption, these women were all white. Through digital collage, this image presents a critique of the continued legacies of computing, highlighting the dependence on data harvested that disproportionally rely upon and systemically discriminate communities of color.

by Shady Sharify

🔍 View Full Screen
Artist(s) Shady Sharify
#20

Who is AI Made Of

by Shady Sharify

🎨 Artist's Statement
This artwork captures humanity’s collective endeavour in building artificial intelligence, drawing inspiration from Persian Negargari (miniature painting). It emphasises that AI is not the result of sudden breakthroughs but centuries of collaboration among minds, cultures, and technologies. Inspired by Kamal-ud-Din Behzad’s late 15th-century painting “Construction of the Khawarnaq Palace” (circa 1494 CE), the piece celebrates human creativity and labor in crafting a structure that reaches toward the heavens. By blending traditional Persian Negargari with modern AI symbols—such as circuit boards, cloud storage, and digital glitches—the artwork underscores that AI is rooted in human effort, shaped over time through the fusion of data, labor, and algorithms, whilst also highlighting the interplay of heritage and technology.

#21

Glitch Binary Abyss I

by Elise Racine & The Bigger Picture

🎨 Artist's Statement
Giant QR code-like patterns dominate the cityscapes, blending seamlessly with the architecture to suggest that algorithmic systems have become intrinsic to the very fabric of urban life. Towering buildings and the street are covered in these black-and-white codes, reflecting how even the most basic aspects of everyday life— where we walk, work, and live — are monitored. The stark black-and-white aesthetic not only underscores the binary nature of these systems but also hints at what may and may not be encoded and, therefore, lost—such as the nuanced “color” and complexity of our world. Ultimately, the piece invites viewers to consider the pervasive nature of AI-powered surveillance systems, how such technologies have come to define public spaces, and whether there is room for the “human” element.

🔍 View Full Screen
Artist(s) Nacho Kamenov & Humans in the Loop
#22

Data annotators discussing the correct labeling of a dataset

by Nacho Kamenov & Humans in the Loop

🎨 Artist's Statement
Data annotation for AI is a subjective activity which involves a lot of interpretative work and communication between the annotators and the data scientists. Sometimes, when you are labeling a training dataset for AI, you bump into a lot of edge cases: occluded objects, blurry images, groups of many objects together, etc. How you annotate them can be crucial for ensuring that the resulting AI system is robust in cases of uncertainty. This is what we call the 'long tail' of AI edge cases and where human involvement is most needed.
